P1Compare and contrast CBOW and skip-gram. In which scenarios would you prefer each?6 marks

Definition. CBOW and skip-gram are the two training architectures of Word2Vec, a shallow neural network that learns a dense vector (usually 100 to 300 numbers) for every word, so that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors. CBOW predicts the target word from its context words; skip-gram predicts the context words from the target word.

Example. Sentence "the quick brown fox jumps over", window 2, centre word "fox". CBOW makes one example: {quick, brown, jumps, over} → fox. Skip-gram makes four: (fox, quick), (fox, brown), (fox, jumps), (fox, over).

When to use which. CBOW: huge corpus of common words and limited compute (product catalogue of frequent items, spoken-command systems). Skip-gram: rare or domain-specific words matter, or the data is small (legal precedents, medical terms).

In gensim the switch is one flag: sg=0 for CBOW, sg=1 for skip-gram.

P2Explain how GloVe creates word embeddings, with an example.3 to 6 marks

Definition. GloVe (Global Vectors) learns word vectors by fitting them to the word-word co-occurrence counts of the whole corpus, so that the dot product of two word vectors approximates the logarithm of how often the two words occur together.

For a 3-mark question write the definition, steps 1 to 3 and the last line. For 6 marks add the example.

  1. Count. Build a matrix X where X_ij is the number of times word j appears in a window around word i, over the entire corpus.
  2. Fit. Learn word vectors w_i, context vectors w̃_j and biases b_i, b̃_j by minimising J = Σ f(X_ij) (w_i·w̃_j + b_i + b̃_j − log X_ij)².
  3. Weight. f(x) = (x / x_max)^0.75 for x < x_max (x_max = 100), otherwise 1. It stops very frequent pairs dominating, and f(0) = 0 so zero counts are skipped.
  4. Output. The final vector of a word is w_i + w̃_i.

Example. Corpus: "I like deep learning", "I like NLP", "I enjoy flying", window 1 (neighbours on both sides). Counts: I-like 2, I-enjoy 1, like-deep 1, like-NLP 1, deep-learning 1, enjoy-flying 1. So the row of "like" is I = 2, deep = 1, NLP = 1.

One term of the loss for a pair with X = 10: f(10) = (10/100)^0.75 = 0.178. If the current vectors give w·w̃ + b + b̃ = 1.5 and log 10 = 2.303, the error is 1.5 − 2.303 = −0.803, squared 0.644, weighted 0.178 × 0.644 = 0.115. Gradient descent changes the vectors to shrink this value for every pair.

Why ratios. GloVe is motivated by ratios of co-occurrence probabilities: P(solid | ice) / P(solid | steam) is large, P(gas | ice) / P(gas | steam) is small, and for a neutral word such as "water" the ratio is near 1. The vectors are fitted so their differences encode these ratios.

Versus Word2Vec. Word2Vec learns from local windows one at a time (predictive); GloVe uses aggregated global counts (count-based). Advantage: uses corpus-wide statistics and pre-trained vectors are available. Limitation: needs the co-occurrence matrix in memory, and each word gets one fixed vector whatever the sentence.

P3Demonstrate the working of Word2Vec for converting words to vectors, with an example.6 marks

Definition. Word2Vec is a two-layer neural network (Mikolov et al.) that turns each word into a dense vector learned from its neighbouring words, using the idea that words in similar contexts have similar meanings.

  1. Prepare. Tokenise, lowercase, build the vocabulary of V words, choose vector size d (say 100), window (say 2) and architecture (CBOW or skip-gram).
  2. Make training pairs by sliding the window over the text.
  3. Network. One-hot input (V) → hidden layer W (V × d, no activation; this is the embedding table) → output layer W′ (d × V) → softmax over the V words.
  4. Train. Minimise cross-entropy (or the negative-sampling loss) with gradient descent over many passes. Words that appear in the same contexts receive the same updates, so their rows in W drift together.
  5. Use. Throw away the output layer and keep the rows of W as the word vectors.

Example. Text "the cat drinks milk", window 2, skip-gram. Pairs for the centre word "drinks": (drinks, the), (drinks, cat), (drinks, milk). Over a large corpus "cat" and "dog" both occur near "drinks", "milk", "pet", so their vectors end up close (cosine similarity near 1), while "cat" and "airplane" stay far apart. The learned space also supports analogies: king − man + woman ≈ queen.

Size. With V = 10,000 and d = 300 there are two matrices of 3,000,000 numbers each, 6,000,000 parameters in total.

Limitation. One vector per word, so "bank" (river) and "bank" (money) share a vector; contextual models such as BERT fix this.

P4Illustrate how word embeddings are generated with the skip-gram model. What are the steps to train skip-gram on a corpus of healthcare data?6 marks (3 marks for the short form)

Definition. Skip-gram takes one centre word and predicts the words around it inside a window; the weights that map the centre word to its hidden layer become its embedding.

Example. Clinical sentence "patient reports chest pain and shortness of breath", window 2, centre "chest". Pairs: (chest, patient), (chest, reports), (chest, pain), (chest, and). Each pair is one training example, so a rare term like "dyspnoea" still produces several examples per occurrence.

Steps for a healthcare corpus

  1. Collect and protect. Clinical notes, discharge summaries or PubMed abstracts. Remove names, IDs and dates (de-identification) before anything else.
  2. Clean. Lowercase, split into sentences and tokens, keep medical abbreviations and units (mg, ECG), replace numbers with a tag, join frequent phrases such as heart_attack.
  3. Build vocabulary. Drop words below min_count (for example 5). Subsample very frequent words ("the", "patient").
  4. Generate pairs with window 5 (clinical text is short and dense, a smaller window favours syntactic similarity, a larger one favours topical similarity).
  5. Train with negative sampling. Word2Vec(sentences, vector_size=100, window=5, min_count=5, sg=1, negative=5), several epochs, decaying learning rate.
  6. Evaluate. most_similar('diabetes') should return related conditions or drugs; check on a downstream task (disease-code classification); plot with PCA or t-SNE.

Why skip-gram here. Medical vocabularies are full of rare terms and the data is small, which is where skip-gram beats CBOW.

P5You are given a corpus of news articles. Illustrate the steps to preprocess the text and train a Word2Vec model on it.6 marks

Goal. Turn raw articles into clean token lists (one list per sentence) and train vectors that capture how words are used in news.

  1. Collect and clean. Remove HTML, bylines, ads, URLs and boilerplate; fix encoding.
  2. Split articles into sentences, then sentences into tokens.
  3. Normalise. Lowercase (but note this merges "Apple" the company with "apple" the fruit), strip punctuation, replace numbers and dates with a tag such as NUM. Stop-word removal is optional because Word2Vec already subsamples frequent words.
  4. Phrases. Detect multi-word names such as new_york or prime_minister so they get one vector.
  5. Vocabulary. Keep words with min_count of at least 5 to drop typos and one-off names.
  6. Train. Word2Vec(sentences, vector_size=200, window=5, min_count=5, sg=1, epochs=10). News corpora are large, so CBOW (sg=0) is a faster alternative.
  7. Evaluate and use. model.wv.most_similar('election') should list votes, polls, ballot; try analogies such as Paris − France + Italy ≈ Rome; save the vectors for classification or search.

Tip. Lemmatising is optional; keep it only if you want "election" and "elections" to share one vector.

P6Elaborate on the negative sampling technique in Word2Vec. How does it improve training efficiency?3 marks

Problem. The softmax output layer must score all V words for every training pair; with V = 100,000 each update touches 100,000 output vectors.

Idea. Turn the task into yes/no questions. For each real (target, context) pair (label 1) pick k random "noise" words (label 0) and update only those 1 + k output vectors. Noise words are drawn from the unigram distribution raised to the power 3/4, so frequent words are sampled less often. Typical k is 5 to 20 for small corpora and 2 to 5 for large ones.

Loss. −log σ(u_o·v_c) − Σ log σ(−u_n·v_c), where v_c is the target vector, u_o the real context and u_n the noise words.

Numbers. Real pair score 2.0, two noise scores −1.0 and −0.5: loss = −log σ(2.0) − log σ(1.0) − log σ(0.5) = 0.127 + 0.313 + 0.474 = 0.914.

Gain. With V = 100,000 and k = 5 only 6 output vectors are updated instead of 100,000, about 16,700 times less work per step, with little or no loss in vector quality.

P7In a sentiment analysis project you must classify tweets as positive, negative or neutral. How would you use Word2Vec embeddings to represent the words and improve the classifier?6 marks

Idea. Replace sparse word counts with dense vectors in which similar words ("good", "great", "superb") sit close together, so the classifier generalises to words it saw rarely.

  1. Clean tweets. Lowercase, replace URLs and @mentions with tags, split hashtags, shorten repeated letters ("soooo" → "soo"), keep or map emojis, keep negation words.
  2. Get embeddings. Load pre-trained Twitter or Google News vectors, or train skip-gram on a large collection of tweets so slang is covered. Out-of-vocabulary words get a zero or random vector (FastText handles them better).
  3. Represent each tweet. Either average its word vectors into one 300-number vector, or feed the sequence of vectors to an LSTM/GRU to keep word order.
  4. Classify. A softmax layer with 3 outputs (positive, negative, neutral). For averaged 300-d input the layer has 300 × 3 + 3 = 903 parameters. Train with cross-entropy and fine-tune the embeddings if there is enough data.
  5. Evaluate with macro-averaged F1, because neutral tweets often dominate.

Why it improves results. A bag-of-words model has no feature for an unseen word like "superb"; embeddings give it a vector close to "excellent", so it still predicts positive.

Limit. Averaging loses order and negation ("not good") and cannot detect sarcasm; use an LSTM or BERT when that matters.

P8Justify the need for word embeddings in AI-based text analysis problems.3 marks

Models work on numbers, so words must be converted to vectors. The simple choice, one-hot encoding, fails in three ways, and embeddings fix each.

  • Size. One-hot vectors have V dimensions (50,000 or more) and are almost all zeros; embeddings use about 300 dense numbers (about 166 times smaller for a 50,000-word vocabulary).
  • Meaning. One-hot vectors are mutually orthogonal, so "cat" is as far from "dog" as from "airplane"; embeddings place related words close (cosine similarity).
  • Generalisation. Embeddings learned from a huge unlabelled corpus can be reused (transfer learning), so a classifier with little labelled data still knows that "buy" and "purchase" are alike.

They also capture relations such as king − man + woman ≈ queen and feed directly into RNN, LSTM and Transformer models.

P9You trained word embeddings on a dataset of m1 words and want to use them for a task that has a separate labelled dataset of m2 words. For the embeddings to help (transfer learning), which must be bigger: m1 ≫ m2 or m2 ≫ m1? Justify.3 marks

Answer: m1 ≫ m2.

  • Embeddings are learned from a very large, cheap, unlabelled corpus (m1, for example billions of words), where they absorb general word meaning and relations.
  • The target task has only a small labelled set (m2, for example thousands of words), too small to learn good word meanings on its own. Reusing the embeddings gives it that knowledge for free, so it needs fewer labelled examples and overfits less.
  • If m2 were much larger than m1, the task data would already teach the model its own representations and the small m1 corpus would add little (it could even be worse than learning from m2).

Example: GloVe vectors trained on Wikipedia and web text (m1) used for a 5,000-review sentiment task (m2).

P10Evaluate how word embeddings have transformed traditional NLP models that were based on feature engineering.6 marks

Before. Traditional NLP models (Naive Bayes, SVM, CRF) depended on hand-designed features: n-gram counts, TF-IDF, POS tags, capitalisation flags, gazetteers (word lists). Every task needed its own feature work, and the features were sparse and had no notion of word similarity.

After. Word embeddings learn a dense vector for each word from raw text, with no manual design.

P11Write a note on LSTM and draw the architecture of an LSTM network.3 marks

Definition. LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) is a recurrent network whose cell keeps a separate memory, the cell state c_t, and uses three gates (forget, input, output) to decide what to erase, write and show. This lets it learn dependencies over many time steps where a plain RNN fails.

          c(t-1) --->( x )-------------->( + )------------+---> c(t)
                       ^                    ^              |
                       | f                  | i * g        v
                       |                  ( x )        [tanh]
                       |                 ^     ^          |
                     [sig]            [sig]  [tanh]       v
                       ^                ^      ^    o --->( x )---> h(t)
                       |                |      |    ^
   h(t-1) , x(t) ------+----------------+------+----+
   f = forget gate   i = input gate   g = candidate memory   o = output gate

How to sketch it: draw a horizontal line on top for the cell state, three small sigmoid boxes below it (forget, input, output) and one tanh box (candidate), all fed by [h_t−1, x_t], with × and + circles where they meet the cell-state line.

  • f_t = σ(W_f[h_t−1, x_t] + b_f), i_t = σ(W_i[h_t−1, x_t] + b_i), o_t = σ(W_o[h_t−1, x_t] + b_o)
  • g_t = tanh(W_g[h_t−1, x_t] + b_g), c_t = f_t ⊙ c_t−1 + i_t ⊙ g_t, h_t = o_t ⊙ tanh(c_t)

Use: language modelling, sentiment analysis, machine translation, speech recognition.

P12Describe the purpose of the gates (input, forget, output) in an LSTM cell and evaluate how they control the flow of information.3 to 6 marks

Definition. A gate is a sigmoid layer whose output lies between 0 (block everything) and 1 (let everything through); it is multiplied element by element with a signal, so it works like a valve.

GateQuestion it answersActs on
Forget f_tHow much of the old memory should be kept?c_t−1
Input i_tHow much of the new candidate g_t should be written?g_t
Output o_tHow much of the memory should be shown as h_t?tanh(c_t)

c_t = f_t ⊙ c_t−1 + i_t ⊙ g_t and h_t = o_t ⊙ tanh(c_t).

Worked step (one unit, x = 1, h_t−1 = 0.5, c_t−1 = 0.4):

  • f = σ(0.5×1 + 0.4×0.5 + 0.1) = σ(0.8) = 0.690
  • i = σ(0.6×1 − 0.2×0.5 + 0) = σ(0.5) = 0.622
  • g = tanh(0.9×1 + 0.3×0.5 + 0.1) = tanh(1.15) = 0.818
  • o = σ(0.7×1 + 0.1×0.5 + 0.2) = σ(0.95) = 0.721
  • c_t = 0.690×0.4 + 0.622×0.818 = 0.276 + 0.509 = 0.785
  • h_t = 0.721 × tanh(0.785) = 0.721 × 0.656 = 0.473

Evaluation. The forget gate lets the cell drop old, irrelevant context (for example a previous sentence's subject); the input gate stops noise from entering memory; the output gate lets the cell hold information privately without exposing it at every step. Because the memory update is additive, the gradient through c_t is multiplied only by f_t, and with f_t close to 1 it flows back almost unchanged, which is what fights vanishing gradients. Cost: four weight blocks, so 4·n_h(n_x + n_h + 1) parameters.

P13Explain the vanishing gradient problem and how LSTM and GRU architectures address it.6 marks

Definition. In an RNN trained by backpropagation through time, the gradient that reaches an early time step is a product of many factors (each made of W_hh and the tanh derivative). When these factors are below 1 the product shrinks exponentially with distance, so the network cannot learn long-range dependencies. This is the vanishing gradient problem (above 1 it explodes).

Numbers. Factor 0.9 per step: 0.9^10 = 0.35 but 0.9^50 = 0.005. The sigmoid derivative is at most 0.25, and 0.25^10 ≈ 1×10^−6. Effect: in "The books that the teacher gave to the students last year were heavy" a plain RNN forgets that the subject was plural.

LSTM fix. The cell state is updated additively, c_t = f_t ⊙ c_t−1 + i_t ⊙ g_t, so ∂c_t/∂c_t−1 = f_t. With f_t ≈ 0.95 the gradient over 50 steps is 0.95^50 = 0.077 instead of 0.005, and a forget gate near 1 gives a nearly constant error path.

GRU fix. Two gates, update z and reset r: h_t = (1 − z_t) ⊙ h_t−1 + z_t ⊙ h̃_t. When z ≈ 0 the old state is copied forward, again an additive path. (Some books swap the roles, h_t = z ⊙ h_t−1 + (1 − z_t) ⊙ h̃_t; state the convention you use.)

Exploding gradients are handled by gradient clipping. LSTM and GRU reduce the problem but do not remove it for very long sequences; attention and Transformers go further.

P14Discuss the techniques used to resolve the vanishing gradient problem in RNNs.3 marks

  • Gated cells (LSTM, GRU): additive state update gives gradients a short, stable path.
  • Better initialisation: orthogonal or identity initialisation of W_hh keeps gradient size near 1 at the start.
  • Activation choice: ReLU-type activations do not squash gradients the way sigmoid (max slope 0.25) and tanh do.
  • Skip or residual connections and normalisation layers give gradients a shortcut.
  • Truncated BPTT limits how far gradients travel (it saves memory, but long-range credit is lost).
  • Attention and Transformers: connect any two positions directly, so the path length is 1.

Note: gradient clipping fixes exploding gradients only, not vanishing ones.

P16How can the LSTM architecture be implemented for language modelling?6 marks

Definition. A language model gives the probability of the next word, P(w_t | w_1 … w_t−1). An LSTM LM reads the words so far and keeps the history in its cell state instead of a fixed n-gram window.

input   :  the     cat     sat     on      the
             |       |       |       |       |
Embedding   v       v       v       v       v
LSTM     [h1] -> [h2] -> [h3] -> [h4] -> [h5]      (state carried forward)
             |       |       |       |       |
Softmax(V)   v       v       v       v       v
target   :  cat     sat     on      the     mat
  1. Data. Tokenise; the target sequence is the input shifted by one word.
  2. Layers. Embedding → LSTM (return a hidden state at every step) → Dense layer of size V with softmax.
  3. Loss. Cross-entropy at every position; metric: perplexity = exp(average cross-entropy).
  4. Generate text. Feed a seed such as "the cat sat on the", pick the next word (highest probability or sampled), append it, repeat.

Parameters (V = 10,000, d = 100, n_h = 128): embedding 1,000,000; LSTM 4 × 128 × (100 + 128 + 1) = 117,248; output 128 × 10,000 + 10,000 = 1,290,000; total 2,407,248. The embedding and softmax layers dominate.

Perplexity example. Average cross-entropy 4.605 gives perplexity e^4.605 = 100: the model is as unsure as picking among 100 equally likely words. Lower is better.

Advantage: no fixed context window and no zero-count problem. Limit: slow softmax for large V, sequential training.

P17Discuss the step-by-step implementation architecture of Bi-LSTM with a suitable example.6 marks

Definition. A Bidirectional LSTM runs one LSTM left-to-right and a second LSTM right-to-left over the same sentence and joins their states, so every word sees both its past and its future.

words      :   He       saw      the      bank     by       the     river
                |        |        |        |        |        |        |
forward  LSTM:  ->       ->       ->       ->       ->       ->       ->
backward LSTM:  <-       <-       <-       <-       <-       <-       <-
                |        |        |        |        |        |        |
concat [fwd;bwd] at each word  ->  Dense + softmax  ->  tag per word
  1. Embed every word into a vector.
  2. Forward LSTM reads word 1 to n and produces h_t→.
  3. Backward LSTM reads word n to 1 and produces h_t←.
  4. Concatenate: h_t = [h_t→ ; h_t←], twice as wide.
  5. Output layer: softmax per word for tagging (POS, NER), or pool/last states then sigmoid for classification.
  6. Train with cross-entropy as for a normal LSTM.

Example. In "He saw the bank by the river" the forward state at "bank" only knows "He saw the"; the backward state knows "by the river", which settles that it is a river bank (a LOCATION or geographic noun), not a money bank.

P18Justify the need for attention in neural networks and differentiate the various types of attention models.6 marks

Need. A basic encoder-decoder squeezes the whole source sentence into one fixed-size context vector. For long sentences information is lost, quality falls, and gradients must travel through many steps. Attention removes this bottleneck: at every output step the decoder scores all encoder states, turns the scores into weights with softmax, and takes the weighted sum as a fresh context vector. It also gives soft word alignments and shortens gradient paths.

Steps. (1) score e_i = score(s, h_i); (2) α_i = exp(e_i) / Σ_j exp(e_j); (3) context c = Σ α_i h_i.

Numbers. Decoder state s = (1, 0), encoder states h_1 = (1, 0), h_2 = (0, 1), h_3 = (1, 1). Dot scores = (1, 0, 1). exp = (2.718, 1, 2.718), sum 6.437, so α = (0.4223, 0.1554, 0.4223). c = 0.4223·(1, 0) + 0.1554·(0, 1) + 0.4223·(1, 1) = (0.8446, 0.5777).

P22Justify the need for Transformers and draw the architecture of the BERT model.6 marks (Part A in the paper, 3 for the diagram and 3 for the need)

P29Describe the purpose of the tanh activation function in an RNN used for language modelling.3 marks

  • tanh squashes the hidden-state update into the range (−1, 1), so the values of h_t cannot grow without limit when the same weights are applied over many steps (stability).
  • It is zero-centred (negative and positive values), which usually trains better than sigmoid, whose outputs are all positive.
  • It adds the non-linearity that lets the RNN model more than a linear function of the past.
  • Example: tanh(−0.15) = −0.149 and tanh(0.85) = 0.691.
  • Its derivative 1 − tanh² is at most 1 (sigmoid's is at most 0.25), but it is close to 0 when the unit saturates, which contributes to vanishing gradients.

P31Define backpropagation. How is it used in training neural networks for NLP?3 marks

Definition. Backpropagation is the algorithm that computes the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight by applying the chain rule from the output layer back to the input, so that gradient descent can update the weights.

Steps in an NLP network (for example a sentiment classifier): (1) forward pass: words → embeddings → hidden layers → prediction; (2) compute loss (cross-entropy); (3) backward pass: chain rule gives gradients for the output layer, hidden layers and also the embedding vectors; (4) update w ← w − η · gradient.

Numbers (one sigmoid neuron: x = 2, w = 0.5, b = 0.1, target y = 1, η = 0.1): z = 0.5×2 + 0.1 = 1.1; a = σ(1.1) = 0.750; loss = −ln 0.750 = 0.287. For sigmoid with cross-entropy ∂L/∂z = a − y = −0.250, so ∂L/∂w = (a − y)·x = −0.500 and ∂L/∂b = −0.250. New w = 0.5 − 0.1×(−0.500) = 0.550; new b = 0.1 + 0.025 = 0.125. The next prediction is higher, so the loss falls.

In RNNs the same idea is run over time (BPTT).

P32Explain with a suitable example how backpropagation is implemented in an RNN.6 marks

Definition. Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) unrolls the RNN over the T steps of the sequence, runs ordinary backpropagation on the unrolled network, and because the weights are shared, adds up the gradient contributions from every time step.

  1. Forward pass: h_t = tanh(w_x x_t + w_h h_t−1), then loss L.
  2. Backward pass: start at the last step; at each earlier step multiply by the local derivative: ∂h_t/∂h_t−1 = w_h (1 − h_t²).
  3. Sum the gradients of the shared weights over all steps, then update.

Example (scalar RNN: w_x = 0.5, w_h = 0.8, h_0 = 0.5, inputs x_1 = x_2 = 1, target 1, loss L = ½(h_2 − 1)²):

  • z_1 = 0.5×1 + 0.8×0.5 = 0.9, h_1 = tanh(0.9) = 0.7163
  • z_2 = 0.5×1 + 0.8×0.7163 = 1.0730, h_2 = tanh(1.0730) = 0.7906, L = ½(−0.2094)² = 0.0219
  • δ_2 = ∂L/∂z_2 = (h_2 − 1)(1 − h_2²) = −0.2094 × 0.3749 = −0.0785
  • δ_1 = δ_2 · w_h · (1 − h_1²) = −0.0785 × 0.8 × 0.4869 = −0.0306
  • ∂L/∂w_h = δ_2 h_1 + δ_1 h_0 = −0.0785×0.7163 + (−0.0306)×0.5 = −0.0562 − 0.0153 = −0.0715
  • Update with η = 0.1: w_h = 0.8 − 0.1×(−0.0715) = 0.8072

The two terms are the contributions from step 2 and step 1, which is why it is called "through time". The factor w_h(1 − h²) is repeated at each earlier step, so over many steps the product shrinks (vanishing) or grows (exploding) which is why LSTM, GRU and gradient clipping are used.

P47A text classifier uses one-hot encoding for words but struggles with a large vocabulary and word similarity. What is its primary limitation?3 marks

Primary limitation: one-hot vectors carry no similarity information and grow with the vocabulary.

  • Each word is a vector of length V (the vocabulary size) with a single 1; for 50,000 words that is a 50,000-number vector, almost all zeros (sparse, memory-heavy).

P51Explain the TF-IDF technique and how it improves upon simple term frequency by capturing the importance of words across multiple documents.6 marks

Definition. TF-IDF (Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency) gives each word a weight that is high when the word is frequent in one document but rare across the collection.

  • TF(t, d) = count of t in d ÷ total words in d
  • IDF(t) = log10(N ÷ DF(t)), where N is the number of documents and DF(t) the number containing t
  • TF-IDF = TF × IDF

Problem with plain TF. It only looks inside one document, so "the" and "is" score highest everywhere even though they say nothing about the topic.

Example. A 100-word document uses "the" 5 times and "python" 5 times, in a collection of N = 1,000 documents where "the" occurs in all 1,000 and "python" in 10. TF is 0.05 for both. IDF(the) = log10(1000/1000) = 0; IDF(python) = log10(1000/10) = 2. TF-IDF: the = 0.05 × 0 = 0; python = 0.05 × 2 = 0.1. TF could not separate them; TF-IDF does.

Uses: keyword extraction, search ranking, document similarity, features for classifiers.

Limits: no word order and no meaning (synonyms count as different words), and the vectors are long and sparse. Variants: sublinear TF (1 + log TF), smoothed IDF, length normalisation.

P54Describe how the Naive Bayes algorithm is implemented for text classification, with an example.6 marks

Definition. Naive Bayes classifies a document by choosing the class c with the highest P(c) × Π P(w_i | c), using Bayes' theorem and the "naive" assumption that words are independent given the class.

  1. Prior: P(c) = documents in class c ÷ total documents.
  2. Likelihood with Laplace smoothing: P(w | c) = (count(w, c) + 1) ÷ (total words in c + V), where V is the vocabulary size. This avoids zero probabilities for unseen words.
  3. Score a new document: P(c) × Π P(w_i | c) over its words (in practice sum logs to avoid underflow).
  4. Decide: the class with the larger score; divide by the sum of scores for a posterior probability.

Example. Training reviews: positive: "great fun movie", "loved great acting"; negative: "boring slow movie", "bad boring plot", "awful acting". Vocabulary V = 10 words. Positive class has 6 words, negative class 8. Priors: P(pos) = 2/5 = 0.4, P(neg) = 3/5 = 0.6.

Test review: "great fun acting".

WordP(w | pos)P(w | neg)
great(2+1)/(6+10) = 3/16(0+1)/(8+10) = 1/18
fun(1+1)/16 = 1/8(0+1)/18 = 1/18
acting(1+1)/16 = 1/8(1+1)/18 = 1/9

Score(pos) = 0.4 × 3/16 × 1/8 × 1/8 = 0.001172. Score(neg) = 0.6 × 1/18 × 1/18 × 1/9 = 0.000206. Posterior P(pos) = 0.001172 / (0.001172 + 0.000206) = 0.85. Predicted class: positive.

With scikit-learn: CountVectorizer() then MultinomialNB(), fit and predict.

Advantage: fast, works with little data. Limit: independence assumption ignores word order and negation ("not good").

P56Which machine learning algorithm is used to implement binary-class text classification?3 marks

P61How is the ROUGE evaluation metric used for NLP model performance analysis?3 marks

Beyond the deck: standard textbook content, not from the course notes.

Definition. ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) compares a machine-generated summary (candidate) with one or more human reference summaries by counting overlapping units. It is mainly used for text summarisation, and also for translation and headline generation.

  • ROUGE-N: overlap of n-grams. ROUGE-1 uses words, ROUGE-2 uses word pairs. Recall = matched n-grams ÷ n-grams in the reference; precision = matched ÷ n-grams in the candidate; F1 combines them.
  • ROUGE-L: based on the longest common subsequence (LCS), which rewards correct word order without needing adjacent words.

Example. Reference: "the cat is on the mat" (6 words). Candidate: "the cat sat on the mat" (6 words).

  • ROUGE-1: matched words = the, cat, on, the, mat = 5; recall = 5/6, precision = 5/6, F1 = 0.833.
  • ROUGE-2: reference bigrams (5): the cat, cat is, is on, on the, the mat; candidate (5): the cat, cat sat, sat on, on the, the mat; matches = the cat, on the, the mat = 3; recall = 3/5 = 0.6, precision = 0.6, F1 = 0.6.
  • ROUGE-L: LCS = "the cat on the mat" = 5 words; recall = precision = 5/6 = 0.833.

BLEU vs ROUGE: BLEU is precision-oriented (is the output correct?), ROUGE is recall-oriented (does it cover the reference?). Limit: counts words, not meaning, so a good paraphrase scores low.

P62Describe any four evaluation metrics used for NLP and explain in detail the BLEU score along with its calculation method. How is BLEU used to assess NLP applications?6 marks

Four metrics. (1) F1 score (precision and recall) for classification and NER; (2) BLEU for machine translation; (3) ROUGE for summarisation; (4) perplexity for language models. (METEOR, which adds stems and synonyms, and chrF, based on character n-grams, are alternatives.)

BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy). It scores a candidate translation against human reference translations using modified n-gram precision and a brevity penalty.

  • Modified precision p_n = clipped matches of n-grams ÷ n-grams in the candidate. Clipping counts a candidate n-gram at most as often as it appears in the reference, so repeating "the the the" cannot cheat.
  • Brevity penalty: BP = 1 if c > r, otherwise e^(1 − r/c), where c is the candidate length and r the reference length. It stops very short outputs scoring well.
  • BLEU = BP × exp(Σ w_n ln p_n), usually N = 4 and w_n = 1/4, a geometric mean of p_1 to p_4.

P63Compare and contrast micro-average and macro-average performance metrics.3 marks

Beyond the deck: standard textbook content, not from the course notes.

Both combine per-class results in multi-class evaluation.

  • Macro-average: compute the metric for each class, then take the plain mean. Every class counts equally.
  • Micro-average: add up TP, FP and FN over all classes, then compute the metric once. Every sample counts equally, so large classes dominate. (For single-label multi-class problems micro-F1 equals accuracy.)

Example. Three classes, 75 test documents (rows = true class, columns = predicted):

SportsPoliticsTech
Sports5055
Politics541
Tech311

Per-class precision: Sports 50/58 = 0.862, Politics 4/10 = 0.400, Tech 1/7 = 0.143. Macro-precision = (0.862 + 0.400 + 0.143) / 3 = 0.468.

Micro: TP = 50 + 4 + 1 = 55, FP = 8 + 6 + 6 = 20, FN = 10 + 6 + 4 = 20. Micro-precision = 55/(55 + 20) = 0.733. Micro-recall is also 55/75 = 0.733 and so is micro-F1.

Macro-F1 = (0.847 + 0.400 + 0.167) / 3 = 0.471.

Reading: micro (0.733) looks good because Sports is large; macro (0.471) exposes that Politics and Tech are handled badly. Use macro when small classes matter, micro when overall performance matters.

P67Explain how topic modelling can be implemented using LSI.6 marks

Beyond the deck: standard textbook content, not from the course notes.

Idea. Build a term-document matrix A (terms × documents) and approximate it with a few strong patterns using truncated SVD: A ≈ U_k Σ_k V_kᵀ. Each of the k kept directions is a topic.

  1. Preprocess: tokenise, remove stop words, lemmatise.
  2. Matrix: count or TF-IDF weights, A of size m terms × n documents.
  3. SVD A = U Σ Vᵀ; keep the k largest singular values.
  4. Read topics: the columns of U_k give term weights for each topic (list the top terms); the rows of V_k (scaled by Σ_k) give each document's coordinates.
  5. Use: compare documents or queries by cosine similarity in the k-dimensional space.

Worked example. Five documents: d1 "car engine", d2 "auto engine", d3 "car auto", d4 "bank money", d5 "money bank money".

d1d2d3d4d5
car10100
auto01100
engine11000
bank00011
money00012

The singular values are 2.618, 2.000, 1.000, 1.000, 0.382. Keep k = 2. Topic 1 (finance): money 0.85, bank 0.53. Topic 2 (vehicles): car, auto, engine 0.58 each. (Signs from SVD are arbitrary; magnitudes matter.) Documents d1, d2, d3 lie on the vehicle axis, d4 and d5 on the finance axis.

Why LSI helps. Query "car". In the original term space its cosine with d2 ("auto engine") is 0, because d2 does not contain the word "car". In the 2-topic space the query lies on the vehicle axis and its cosine with d2 is 1.0. LSI therefore finds documents about the same idea even without the exact word.

Limit: k must be chosen, topics can have negative weights, and polysemous words are mixed.

P69Explain the applications of language modelling in various domains.3 marks

A language model assigns a probability to a sequence of words, P(w_1 … w_n), or predicts the next word. A system picks the more probable, more fluent text.

  • Speech recognition: chooses "recognise speech" over the acoustically similar "wreck a nice beach" because it is far more probable.

  • Auto-complete and predictive text: suggests the next word in keyboards and search boxes.
  • Spelling and grammar correction: prefers "their car" to "there car".
  • Text generation and chatbots: GPT-style models generate answers, stories, code and summaries.
  • Information retrieval, OCR, summarisation: rank candidates and pick the likelier reading.

P70Discuss smoothing in probabilistic language models. Comment on its need.3 marks

Need. Maximum-likelihood estimates give probability 0 to any n-gram unseen in training: P(w_i | w_i−1) = C(w_i−1, w_i) / C(w_i−1). A sentence's probability is the product of its n-gram probabilities, so one zero makes the whole sentence impossible (data sparsity). Smoothing takes a little probability from seen events and gives it to unseen ones.

Laplace (add-one): P = (C(w_i−1, w_i) + 1) / (C(w_i−1) + V).

Example (corpus "I enjoy reading books", "I enjoy watching movies", "I enjoy reading news"; V = 9 tokens including the two boundary markers; C(reading) = 2). P(books | reading) = 1/2 by MLE. The unseen pair "reading movies" has MLE 0, but with add-one it gets (0+1)/(2+9) = 1/11 = 0.091. Cost: seen pairs shrink, for instance (1+1)/(2+9) = 0.18 instead of 0.5.

Other methods: add-k, Good-Turing, backoff, interpolation, Kneser-Ney (used in practice).

P72Describe dependency parsing. Why is it important in NLP?3 marks

Beyond the deck: standard textbook content, not from the course notes.

Definition. Dependency parsing finds the grammatical structure of a sentence as directed, labelled links from a head word to its dependents. One word (usually the main verb) is the root and every other word depends on exactly one head.

Example. "She bought a red car":

bought (root)
 |-- She  (nsubj)    subject of the verb
 |-- car  (obj)      object of the verb
      |-- a    (det)    determiner
      |-- red  (amod)   adjective modifier

Methods. Transition-based parsers read left to right using a stack and the actions SHIFT, LEFT-ARC and RIGHT-ARC (for "She bought cars": shift, shift, left-arc (bought → She), shift, right-arc (bought → cars), right-arc (root → bought)). Graph-based parsers score all possible arcs and choose the best tree. Neural parsers (BiLSTM or Transformer based) now give the best accuracy.

Importance. It shows who did what to whom, so it supports information and relation extraction, question answering, machine translation (word reordering), aspect-based sentiment (which noun an adjective describes) and grammar checking. Unlike a constituency tree, it directly links words, and it is more suited to free-word-order languages such as Hindi.

P73Explain the term "paradigm shift" in the context of modern NLP.3 marks

Beyond the deck: standard textbook content, not from the course notes.

A paradigm shift is a change in the basic way a field works. In NLP the shift has been from hand-written rules and hand-made features to learning everything from data, and then to one large pre-trained model reused for many tasks.

StageApproachExamples
Rule-based (1950s to 1980s)hand-written grammar and dictionariesrule-based translation
Statistical (1990s to 2000s)probabilities from data plus hand-engineered featuresn-grams, HMM, Naive Bayes, SVM, statistical MT
Neural (2013 onward)learned dense representations, sequence modelsWord2Vec, RNN/LSTM, seq2seq, attention
Pre-trained Transformers (2018 onward)pre-train on huge text, then fine-tune or promptBERT, GPT, large language models

Key changes: rules → data; feature engineering → representation learning; static word vectors → contextual ones; a separate model per task → one pre-trained model adapted to many tasks (transfer learning, prompting).
