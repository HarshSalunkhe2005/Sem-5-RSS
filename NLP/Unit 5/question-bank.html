Unit 5: Machine Translation & Advanced NLP (CO5)

Symbiosis Institute of Technology — Course Code TEE7101

Q1Apply Seq2Seq model for a simple translation task and interpret results.6 marks

Setup: parallel pairs such as "I love NLP" → "मुझे NLP पसंद है". Tokenise, add <sos>/<eos>, build source and target vocabularies, embed. Encoder LSTM reads the source and its final state is the context vector c; decoder LSTM starts from c and <sos> and emits one word per step through a softmax. Train with teacher forcing and cross-entropy loss; at test time decode with greedy or beam search until <eos>.

Interpreting results: falling training/validation loss shows learning; compare outputs against references with BLEU. Typical findings: short sentences are translated well, long ones degrade (fixed context-vector bottleneck), rare words become <unk>, and outputs can be fluent but wrong. Adding attention and viewing the alignment matrix shows which source word each output word relied on and usually raises BLEU on long sentences.

Q2Analyze the working of encoder-decoder architecture in machine translation.6 marks

The encoder processes source tokens x₁…x_n sequentially, h_t = f(x_t, h_{t−1}), compressing the sentence into a context vector c (the final state). The decoder is a conditional language model: s_t = f(s_{t−1}, y_{t−1}, c) and P(y_t | y_<t, x) = softmax(W s_t). The whole translation probability is P(y|x) = ∏ P(y_t | y_<t, x), trained end to end by maximising log-likelihood (teacher forcing).

Analysis: strength is that variable-length input maps to variable-length output with no hand-made features. Weakness is the single fixed-size c, which is a bottleneck for long sentences and makes training depend on long gradient paths. Attention fixes this by giving the decoder a fresh weighted combination of all encoder states at each step.

Q4Evaluate the performance of transformer-based models over RNNs.6 marks

Transformer advantages: (1) fully parallel over positions, O(1) sequential operations vs O(n) for RNN, so far faster training on GPUs; (2) path length between any two words is O(1) (O(n) in an RNN), so long-range dependencies are captured better and gradients do not vanish; (3) multi-head attention models several relations at once; (4) scales to huge data and pre-training (BERT, GPT); (5) higher BLEU in the original paper (28.4 on WMT14 EN-DE) at a fraction of training cost.

Drawbacks: self-attention costs O(n²·d) memory/time so very long inputs are expensive; needs positional encoding as it has no built-in order; data-hungry. Verdict: Transformers outperform RNNs for MT and almost all NLP tasks; RNNs remain only for tiny/streaming settings.

Q5Given a translation problem, design an appropriate NLP solution.6 marks

Example: English to Marathi for a government notice service. 1 Define: domain (formal), quality/latency needs. 2 Data: collect parallel corpus (+ monolingual Marathi), clean, deduplicate, normalise Devanagari. 3 Tokenise: SentencePiece/BPE joint vocabulary. 4 Model: fine-tune a pre-trained multilingual Transformer (low-resource target); augment with back-translation. 5 Train: teacher forcing, label smoothing. 6 Decode: beam search k = 4-5 with length normalisation. 7 Evaluate: BLEU and chrF on held-out set plus human review; add glossary for official terms. 8 Deploy and monitor: post-editing loop, collect corrections to retrain.

Q6Analyze challenges in multilingual machine translation.6 marks

  • Low-resource languages: very little parallel data, so poor quality.
  • Language imbalance: high-resource languages dominate training; mitigated by temperature sampling.
  • Limited capacity: more languages compete for the same parameters ("curse of multilinguality"); larger models needed.
  • Shared vocabulary: subwords over-segment low-resource languages and different scripts share few tokens.
  • Linguistic diversity: word order, morphology, scripts.
  • Zero-shot errors: off-target language output, lower quality than supervised pairs.
  • Evaluation and bias: few good test sets, BLEU unsuitable for rich morphology (use chrF), cultural/gender bias.

Q7Evaluate recent advancements in NLP models for language tasks.6 marks

The shift is to pre-train then fine-tune Transformers. BERT (bidirectional encoder, masked LM) lifted understanding tasks. GPT (decoder, next-token prediction) enabled fluent generation and, at scale, few-shot prompting. ELECTRA replaced masked-token prediction with replaced-token detection, learning from every token and so training more efficiently. T5/mT5/BART unify tasks as text-to-text. Multilingual models (mBERT, mT5, M2M/NLLB-style) extend to many languages. LLMs add instruction tuning, RLHF, chain-of-thought prompting, parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA) and retrieval-augmented generation.

Evaluation: large accuracy gains and less task-specific engineering; but heavy compute and energy cost, hallucination, bias, opacity and privacy concerns, so careful evaluation and human oversight are needed.

Q8Given a real-world scenario, justify the choice of NLP model and approach.6 marks

Method: state requirements (data volume, languages, latency, accuracy, budget), then match. Examples: (a) E-commerce review sentiment, 5,000 labelled reviews → fine-tune BERT/ELECTRA (bidirectional context, small labelled data enough). (b) Real-time EN-HI subtitles with lots of parallel data → Transformer NMT with beam search (quality and parallel speed). (c) Marathi-English with few pairs → multilingual pre-trained model + back-translation (transfer from Hindi). (d) Customer-support chatbot, no training set → LLM with few-shot prompt or RAG over company documents. (e) Legal/medical translation → domain fine-tuned NMT with human post-editing, since errors are costly. Always justify by data, risk and cost, not by model popularity.

Practice · Machine Translation Basics & Seq2Seq

Traditional MT, Encoder-Decoder, Decoding

Q9Define machine translation and parallel corpus. Name the three traditional MT approaches.3 marks

Machine translation is the automatic conversion of text from a source language to a target language. A parallel corpus is a collection of sentence pairs, each with the same meaning in two languages, used to train statistical and neural models. Traditional approaches: rule-based (dictionaries + grammar rules; direct, transfer, interlingua), example-based (reuse stored translated examples) and statistical / phrase-based (probabilities learned from the corpus).

Q10Explain the noisy-channel model of statistical MT and the roles of its two factors.4 marks

Treat the foreign sentence f as a noisy version of an English sentence e. By Bayes' rule ê = argmax_e P(e|f) = argmax_e P(f|e)·P(e), dropping P(f) as constant. P(e) is the language model, which favours fluent English; P(f|e) is the translation model learned from alignments, which favours faithful translations. A decoder searches for the e maximising the product. Fluency and adequacy are thus modelled separately.

Q11What is teacher forcing? Why can it be a problem at inference time?3 marks

In teacher forcing the decoder input at step t during training is the ground-truth previous word y_{t−1}, not the model's own prediction. It speeds up and stabilises training and allows parallel computation of all steps in a Transformer. At inference the true words are unavailable, so the model feeds on its own (possibly wrong) outputs; this train/test mismatch is exposure bias, and one early mistake can propagate. Remedies: scheduled sampling, beam search, sequence-level training.

Q12Greedy vs beam search: step 1 gives P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4. Then P(X|A) = 0.4 (best after A) and P(Y|B) = 0.9 (best after B). Find the sequence chosen by greedy and by beam width 2.4 marks

Greedy: picks A (0.5) then X: 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.20 (ln = −1.609). Beam k = 2: keeps both A (0.5) and B (0.4) after step 1; after step 2 candidates are A X = 0.20 and B Y = 0.4 × 0.9 = 0.36 (ln = −1.022). Best is B Y, which greedy missed because it committed to the locally best first word. Beam search costs about k times more and still does not guarantee the global optimum. Scores are summed as log-probabilities and length-normalised.

Q13Explain the context-vector bottleneck of the basic seq2seq model and how it is addressed.4 marks

The encoder must squeeze an entire sentence, whether 5 or 50 words, into one fixed-size vector. Information (especially from early words) is lost, so translation quality falls as sentence length grows, and gradients must flow back through many steps. Solutions: (1) attention, letting the decoder re-read all encoder states with a weighted sum at each step; (2) bidirectional encoder; (3) reversing the source sentence (an early trick); (4) Transformers, which connect all positions directly.

Q14True or False: in encoder-decoder NMT without attention, the decoder has access to every encoder hidden state at every step.1 mark

False. Without attention the decoder only receives the final encoder state (the context vector). Access to all encoder states at each step is exactly what attention adds.

Practice · Attention & Transformer

Attention Scores, Self-Attention, Positional Encoding, Complexity

Q15Decoder state s = (1, 0); encoder states h₁ = (1, 0), h₂ = (0, 1), h₃ = (1, 1). Using dot-product scores, compute the attention weights and the context vector.5 marks

Scores: e = (s·h₁, s·h₂, s·h₃) = (1, 0, 1). Softmax: exp = (2.718, 1, 2.718), sum = 6.437, so α = (0.4223, 0.1554, 0.4223) (sum = 1). Context: c = 0.4223·(1,0) + 0.1554·(0,1) + 0.4223·(1,1) = (0.4223 + 0.4223, 0.1554 + 0.4223) = (0.8446, 0.5777). Interpretation: the decoder attends equally to h₁ and h₃ and little to h₂.

Q16Scaled dot-product attention with d_k = 4: q = (1,0,1,0); k₁ = (1,0,1,0), k₂ = (0,1,0,1), k₃ = (1,1,1,1); v₁ = (1,0), v₂ = (0,1), v₃ = (1,1). Compute the output and say why we divide by √d_k.5 marks

Dot products: q·k = (2, 0, 2). Scale by √4 = 2 → (1, 0, 1). Softmax: (0.4223, 0.1554, 0.4223). Output = 0.4223·v₁ + 0.1554·v₂ + 0.4223·v₃ = (0.8446, 0.5777). Without scaling the softmax of (2,0,2) is (0.468, 0.063, 0.468), sharper. Reason: for large d_k the dot products have large variance (≈ d_k), pushing softmax into saturated regions with tiny gradients; dividing by √d_k keeps the variance near 1.

Q17Why do Transformers need positional encoding? Compute the sinusoidal encoding of positions 1 and 2 for d_model = 4.5 marks

Self-attention is permutation-invariant: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would give the same set of vectors. A position vector is added to each word embedding to inject order. Formula: PE(pos,2i) = sin(pos/10000^(2i/d)), PE(pos,2i+1) = cos(pos/10000^(2i/d)). For d = 4, the divisors are 10000⁰ = 1 (i = 0) and 10000^(2/4) = 100 (i = 1).

pos 1: (sin 1, cos 1, sin 0.01, cos 0.01) = (0.8415, 0.5403, 0.0100, 1.0000). pos 2: (sin 2, cos 2, sin 0.02, cos 0.02) = (0.9093, −0.4161, 0.0200, 0.9998). Low-index dimensions change quickly with position, high-index dimensions slowly, making each position unique. Learned position embeddings are an alternative.

Q18Compare per-layer complexity of self-attention and recurrence for n = 50, d = 512, and estimate the parameters of one Transformer-base encoder layer.5 marks

Complexity per layer: self-attention O(n²·d) = 50² × 512 = 1.28 million operations, sequential ops O(1), max path length O(1). Recurrent layer O(n·d²) = 50 × 512² = 13.1 million, sequential ops O(n), path length O(n). So for n < d self-attention is cheaper and parallel; for very long sequences (n > d) the n² term dominates and attention becomes costlier.

Encoder layer parameters (d = 512, d_ff = 2048): attention W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O = 4 × 512 × 512 = 1,048,576 weights (+2,048 biases) = 1,050,624; feed-forward 512×2048 + 2048×512 = 2,097,152 weights (+2,560 biases) = 2,099,712; two layer-norms 2,048. Total ≈ 3.15 million per encoder layer (a decoder layer has an extra attention block, ≈ 4.2M), so 6 + 6 layers ≈ 44M parameters plus embeddings.

Q19What are multi-head attention and masked self-attention, and where is each used?4 marks

Multi-head attention runs h attention functions in parallel on lower-dimensional projections (8 heads of 64 dims in the base model), concatenates their outputs and projects again; different heads capture different relations (syntax, co-reference, position). It is used in encoder self-attention, decoder self-attention and encoder-decoder (cross) attention. Masked self-attention sets scores for future positions to −∞ before the softmax so token t cannot see tokens after t; it is used only in the decoder, to preserve the left-to-right generation property during parallel training.

Practice · Evaluation, Multilingual & Recent Models

BLEU, Zero-shot, ELECTRA

Q20Reference: "the cat is on the mat". Candidate: "the cat sat on the mat". Compute unigram, bigram and trigram precision, and BLEU-2.6 marks

Unigrams: candidate the, cat, sat, on, the, mat; matches (clipped) the, cat, on, the, mat = 5 of 6 → p₁ = 0.833. Bigrams: the cat, cat sat, sat on, on the, the mat; matches: the cat, on the, the mat = 3 of 5 → p₂ = 0.600. Trigrams: the cat sat, cat sat on, sat on the, on the mat; match: on the mat = 1 of 4 → p₃ = 0.250. 4-grams: 0 of 3 → p₄ = 0 (so BLEU-4 = 0 without smoothing).

Lengths equal (c = r = 6) so BP = 1. BLEU-2 = 1 × exp(½ ln 0.833 + ½ ln 0.6) = √(0.833 × 0.6) = √0.5 = 0.707.

Q21Explain clipping and the brevity penalty in BLEU with numbers. Candidate "the cat on the mat" against the reference "the cat is on the mat": find BLEU-2.5 marks

Clipping: a candidate n-gram is counted at most as often as it appears in the reference. Candidate "the the the the the the the" vs reference with "the" twice → p₁ = 2/7 instead of 7/7, so repetition cannot inflate the score. Brevity penalty: precision alone rewards very short outputs, so BP = 1 if c > r, else exp(1 − r/c).

Here c = 5, r = 6. Unigrams: all 5 match → p₁ = 5/5 = 1. Bigrams: the cat, cat on, on the, the mat; matches: the cat, on the, the mat = 3 of 4 → p₂ = 0.75. BP = exp(1 − 6/5) = exp(−0.2) = 0.819. BLEU-2 = 0.819 × √(1 × 0.75) = 0.819 × 0.866 = 0.709 (versus 0.866 with no penalty). Limits of BLEU: ignores synonyms and meaning; METEOR and chrF are alternatives.

Q22Explain ELECTRA's replaced-token detection and how it differs from BERT's masked language modelling; also explain zero-shot translation in a multilingual model.6 marks

BERT (MLM): mask about 15% of tokens and predict only those, so only 15% of positions give a training signal. ELECTRA: a small generator (masked LM) replaces masked tokens with plausible words; the main model, the discriminator, classifies every token as original or replaced. The loss covers all positions, so ELECTRA learns more per example and reaches a given accuracy with less compute than BERT. After pre-training only the discriminator is kept and fine-tuned. It is not a GAN: the generator is trained by maximum likelihood.

Zero-shot translation: a multilingual NMT model (language tag such as <2mr> on the source) trained on EN↔HI and EN↔MR can translate HI→MR even though it never saw HI-MR pairs, because it learns shared cross-lingual representations. Quality is lower than for supervised pairs and the model may output the wrong language.
