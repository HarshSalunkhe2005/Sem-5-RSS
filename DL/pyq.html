Optimizers and gradient descent variants

Seen in: Dec 23, Dec 24, May 24, May 25, May 26

P1Compare and contrast momentum, Adagrad, RMSprop and Adam, with practical examples of how their optimization behaviour differs.5 to 7 marks

All four start from plain gradient descent, θ ← θ − η·g. Momentum fixes the direction (zig-zag), Adagrad and RMSprop fix the step size per parameter, and Adam does both.

  • Momentum gathers speed: it is the furthest along after two steps (1.84 against 2.56 for plain GD).
  • Adagrad and Adam take a first step of exactly η (4 → 3.9), whatever the size of the gradient. They are cautious here because η = 0.1 is small; they pay off when gradients differ a lot between parameters.

When to use: Adam as the default; RMSprop is a good choice for RNNs; Adagrad for sparse features; momentum when the loss surface is a long narrow valley.

P2Distinguish Batch Gradient Descent, Stochastic Gradient Descent and Mini-batch Gradient Descent (and Gradient Descent vs SGD).3 to 4 marks

All three use the update θ ← θ − η·∇L. They differ only in how many examples are used to estimate the gradient for one update. Plain "gradient descent" in Dec 2024 means the batch version; SGD is the one-example version.

P3What is mini-batch gradient descent in the context of backpropagation?2 marks

Mini-batch gradient descent splits the training set into small batches and performs one backpropagation pass and one weight update per batch, instead of per example or per full dataset.

  1. Shuffle the data and cut it into batches of m examples (for example 64).
  2. Forward pass for the whole batch and average the loss over the m examples.
  3. Backpropagate once to get the average gradient (1/m)Σ∇Lᵢ.
  4. Update: θ ← θ − η·(1/m)Σ∇Lᵢ. Repeat for every batch; one pass over all batches is one epoch.

Example: N = 1000, m = 100 gives 10 updates per epoch, so 100 updates in 10 epochs. The matrix operations for 100 examples run together on a GPU, and the averaged gradient is smoother than a single-example one.

P5What problem does the momentum optimizer address, and how does it do so? (What is the role of momentum?)2 to 4 marks

Problem: on a loss surface that is steep in one direction and shallow in another (a narrow valley), plain gradient descent zig-zags across the steep walls and moves slowly along the valley floor. It is also slow on flat regions and plateaus, and SGD gradients are noisy.

How momentum helps: it keeps a velocity, an exponentially decaying average of past gradients.

  • v ← βv + η·g, then θ ← θ − v (β ≈ 0.9, so the memory is about 1/(1−β) = 10 steps).
  • Gradient components that keep the same sign add up and the weights speed up along the valley.
  • Components that flip sign (the zig-zag) cancel each other.

Example (f = w², start w = 4, η = 0.1, β = 0.9): step 1: g = 8, v = 0.8, w = 3.2. Step 2: g = 6.4, v = 0.9×0.8 + 0.1×6.4 = 1.36, w = 3.2 − 1.36 = 1.84. Plain GD would be at 2.56.

Limitation: one more hyperparameter (β), and the weights can overshoot the minimum before settling.

P6Explain Momentum and Adagrad in the context of training neural networks, with a detailed comparison of their mechanisms, advantages and limitations.4 to 5 marks

Momentum speeds up and smooths the direction of travel; Adagrad adapts the step size of each parameter. Update lines: Momentum v ← βv + η·g, θ ← θ − v. Adagrad G ← G + g², θ ← θ − η·g / (√G + ε).

AdvantageFaster along consistent slopes, less zig-zag, helps through flat regionsNo manual per-feature tuning; rare features get large updates, so good for sparse data
LimitationExtra β; can overshoot; same η for every parameterG only grows, so the rate decays towards zero and learning stalls on long runs

Conclusion: Momentum is better on narrow valleys; Adagrad is better for sparse features; Adam combines both ideas.

P7Discuss how the learning rate affects the convergence of training.3 marks

The learning rate η is the step length in θ ← θ − η·∇L. It is usually the most important hyperparameter.

  • Too small: convergence is correct but very slow, and training may stop on a plateau.
  • Too large: the weights overshoot the minimum, the loss oscillates or diverges (NaN).
  • Just right: the loss falls steadily and settles near the minimum.

Example (f = w², start w = 4, so each step multiplies w by 1 − 2η), value of w after 5 steps:

ηw after 5 stepsBehaviour
0.013.6157too slow

Remedies: try η on a log scale (1e-5 to 1e-1), use a schedule (step decay, cosine, reduce-on-plateau), a short warm-up, or an adaptive optimizer such as Adam (default 1e-3).

P8What is the vanishing gradient problem in deep learning?2 marks

The vanishing gradient problem is the shrinking of the gradient towards zero as it is propagated backwards, so the early layers of a deep network learn extremely slowly or not at all.

  • Cause: by the chain rule the gradient of an early layer is a product of many per-layer factors. For a sigmoid the derivative is at most 0.25, so ten layers give at most 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 9.5×10⁻⁷.
  • Effect: early layers barely change, the loss plateaus, training stalls.
  • Fixes: ReLU-type activations, He or Xavier initialization, batch normalization, residual connections.

P9Discuss the effects of vanishing and exploding gradients on training and the potential solutions to mitigate them.4 to 5 marks

In a deep network the gradient reaching an early layer is a product of many per-layer factors. If the factors are mostly below 1 the product shrinks to almost 0 (vanishing); if mostly above 1 it grows without bound (exploding).

Numbers: sigmoid gradient factor 0.25 over 10 layers: 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 9.5×10⁻⁷. A factor of 1.5 over 10 layers: 1.5¹⁰ ≈ 57.7; over 50 layers ≈ 6×10⁸.

P10State the challenges in training very deep networks.2 to 3 marks

  1. Vanishing or exploding gradients: products of many factors (0.25¹⁰ ≈ 9.5×10⁻⁷); fix with ReLU, He initialization, batch norm.
  2. Degradation: beyond some depth a plain network can show higher training error; fix with residual (skip) connections as in ResNet.
  3. Overfitting: millions of parameters can memorise the data; fix with more data, augmentation, dropout, L2, early stopping.
  4. High computation, memory and training time: cost grows with every layer; needs GPUs or TPUs.
  5. Needs large labelled datasets and is sensitive to hyperparameters (learning rate, initialization, depth, width).

P11Explain the working of a Recurrent Neural Network and how it handles sequential (time-series) data.5 to 7 marks

A Recurrent Neural Network is a network with a hidden state that is fed back into itself, so the output at time t depends on the current input and on a summary of all earlier inputs. The same weights are used at every time step.

Equations (hidden state s, input x, output y):

  • s_t = tanh(U·x_t + W·s_(t−1) + b)
  • y_t = softmax(V·s_t + c)
  • U maps the input, W is the recurrence (memory), V maps the state to the output; U, W, V, b, c are shared across all time steps.
  x1          x2          x3
   |           |           |
   v           v           v
 [ s1 ] ---> [ s2 ] ---> [ s3 ]   same U, W, V at every step
   |           |           |
   v           v           v
  y1          y2          y3

How to sketch: draw one box with a loop arrow on it, then "unroll" it into one box per time step joined left to right.

Worked example (one unit, s₀ = 0, s_t = tanh(0.5·x_t + 0.8·s_(t−1)), inputs 1, 0.5, −1): s₁ = tanh(0.5) = 0.4621; s₂ = tanh(0.25 + 0.8×0.4621) = 0.5509; s₃ = tanh(−0.5 + 0.8×0.5509) = −0.0592. Each state carries information from every earlier input.

Why it suits sequences:

  1. y_t depends on x₁ ... x_t through the state (order matters).
  2. It accepts sequences of any length (the same step is repeated).
  3. Parameter sharing keeps the size fixed: with input size 4, hidden size 3 and output size 2 the count is U 12 + W 9 + b 3 + V 6 + c 2 = 32, however long the sequence.

Tasks: sequence classification (use the last state, for example sentiment), sequence labelling (output at every step, for example tagging), sequence generation (translation). Training uses backpropagation through time (BPTT).

Limitation: gradients vanish over long ranges, which LSTM fixes.

P12Discuss the vanishing gradient problem in RNNs. How are RNNs well suited to tasks involving sequences or time series?5 marks

In an RNN, backpropagation through time sends the gradient back through every time step. The gradient reaching step k is a product of Jacobians:

∂s_T/∂s_k = Π ∂s_j/∂s_(j−1), and each factor contains W and the activation derivative (σ′ ≤ 0.25, tanh′ ≤ 1).

  • If the factors are below 1 the product shrinks exponentially: 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 10⁻⁶, 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ 0.005.
  • So inputs far in the past get almost no learning signal, and the RNN cannot learn long-range dependencies (for example linking a subject to its verb ten words later).
  • If the factors are above 1 the gradient explodes (1.5⁵⁰ ≈ 6×10⁸); this is handled by gradient clipping.

Remedies: LSTM or GRU (additive cell state with gates), gradient clipping, good initialization, truncated BPTT.

Why RNNs still suit sequences: the hidden state is a memory, the weights are shared over time, and the input length can vary. This works well for short and medium dependencies in text, speech and sensor series; for long ones use LSTM.

P13Provide a step-by-step walkthrough of the LSTM architecture and explain the role of each component, including the forget, input and output gates.7 marks

An LSTM is an RNN cell with a cell state c_t (long-term memory) and three gates, each a sigmoid giving values between 0 and 1, that control what is forgotten, written and revealed. (The course notes call the cell state s_t and use σ as the squashing function; the structure is the same.)

  c(t-1) -----> [ x f ] ------> [ + ] ---------+--------> c(t)
                   ^               ^             |
                   |            [ x i ]        tanh
              forget gate         ^              |
                f(t)           input gate        v
                              i(t) * c~(t)    [ x o ] ---> h(t)
                                                 ^
                                            output gate o(t)

  every gate and c~(t) is computed from [ h(t-1), x(t) ]

How to sketch: a horizontal line for c_t across the top, three gate boxes feeding multiply (×) and add (+) nodes, h_t leaving at the bottom right.

  1. Forget gate: f_t = σ(W_f[h_(t−1), x_t] + b_f). Decides how much of the old cell state to keep (1 = keep, 0 = erase).
  2. Input gate and candidate: i_t = σ(W_i[h_(t−1), x_t] + b_i), c̃_t = tanh(W_c[h_(t−1), x_t] + b_c). The candidate is the new information; the gate decides how much of it is written.
  3. Cell update: c_t = f_t ⊙ c_(t−1) + i_t ⊙ c̃_t (keep part of the old, add part of the new).
  4. Output gate: o_t = σ(W_o[h_(t−1), x_t] + b_o), and the hidden state h_t = o_t ⊙ tanh(c_t) is passed on to the next step and to the output layer.

Worked example (one unit): f = 0.8, i = 0.5, o = 0.9, c̃ = 0.6, c_(t−1) = 1.0. Then c_t = 0.8×1.0 + 0.5×0.6 = 1.1 and h_t = 0.9 × tanh(1.1) = 0.9 × 0.8005 ≈ 0.72.

Parameters: four blocks (three gates and the candidate), each n(n + d) + n, so 4[n(n + d) + n]. For n = 3, d = 4: 4×(21 + 3) = 96, four times a plain RNN's 24.

Why it fixes vanishing gradients: the cell update is additive, so ∂c_t/∂c_(t−1) is essentially f_t. When f_t ≈ 1 the gradient flows back through time almost unchanged.

P14Explain the functions of any two gates in an LSTM.2 to 3 marks

  • Forget gate: f_t = σ(W_f[h_(t−1), x_t] + b_f) gives a value between 0 and 1 for each part of the old cell state. It decides what to keep (1) or erase (0). Example: forget the old subject when a new one appears.
  • Input gate: i_t = σ(W_i[h_(t−1), x_t] + b_i) decides how much of the new candidate c̃_t is written to the cell state. Example: store a sentiment word such as "awesome", ignore filler words.

Together they update the memory: c_t = f_t ⊙ c_(t−1) + i_t ⊙ c̃_t. With f = 0.8, i = 0.5, c̃ = 0.6, c_(t−1) = 1.0 this gives 1.1. (The third gate, the output gate, controls how much of tanh(c_t) is shown as h_t.)

P19What is deep learning and how does it fit within the broader field of artificial intelligence? What are Artificial Neural Networks and how do they relate to deep learning?2 marks

Deep learning is a subset of machine learning that uses neural networks with many hidden layers to learn features and predictions directly from raw data. Machine learning is itself a subset of artificial intelligence.

+-------------------------------------------------+
| AI: machines doing tasks that need intelligence |
|  +-----------------------------------------+    |

|  |  +-----------------------------------+  |    |
|  |  | DL: many-layer neural networks    |  |    |
|  |  +-----------------------------------+  |    |

An Artificial Neural Network (ANN) is a set of connected neurons arranged in an input layer, one or more hidden layers and an output layer; each neuron computes z = Σwᵢxᵢ + b and passes it through an activation f(z). An ANN with one hidden layer is shallow; stacking many hidden layers gives a deep network, which is what deep learning means.

P20What are the primary reasons for using deep neural networks in machine learning applications?3 marks

  1. Automatic feature learning: each layer builds more abstract features (pixels → edges → shapes → objects), so no manual feature engineering.
  2. Higher accuracy on complex data: images, speech and text, where deep models beat hand-engineered features (ImageNet error fell from about 26% to 16% in 2012).
  3. Compact representation: depth reuses and composes features, which is more parameter-efficient than one very wide layer.
  4. Scales with data and compute: accuracy keeps improving with more data and GPUs; pretrained networks can be reused for new tasks (transfer learning).

P21Discuss any three reasons for the growing interest in Deep Learning, provide any two key applications across industries, and specify any two challenges faced when implementing deep learning models.7 marks

A. Three reasons for the growing interest

  1. Data: large labelled datasets such as ImageNet (1.2 million images, 1000 classes), web text and sensor data give networks enough examples.
  2. Compute: GPUs and TPUs run the huge matrix multiplications in parallel, giving roughly 10 to 100 times speed-up over CPUs.
  3. Algorithms and automatic features: ReLU, dropout, batch normalization, residual connections and the Transformer made deep networks trainable, and the network learns its own features from raw data.

B. Two key applications

  • Healthcare: CNNs flag tumours, fractures or diabetic retinopathy in scans, for example screening thousands of X-rays a day for a radiologist to review.
  • Banking and finance: real-time fraud detection by learning evolving misuse patterns, scoring each card transaction in milliseconds. (Also: self-driving perception, translation and chatbots, recommendations.)

C. Two challenges

  • Data hungry: needs thousands to millions of labelled examples; accuracy drops sharply with small data.
  • Computationally expensive and a black box: large GPU or TPU time and energy for training, and predictions are hard to interpret (explainable-AI methods such as Grad-CAM and SHAP are used). Others: overfitting and bias, adversarial examples.

P26What is the chain rule, and how is it used in backpropagation?2 marks

Chain rule: if y = f(u) and u = g(x), then dy/dx = (dy/du)·(du/dx). For a chain of functions, multiply the derivatives of each link.

In backpropagation: the loss depends on a weight through a chain z = wx + b, a = σ(z), L = (a − y)². So

∂L/∂w = (∂L/∂a)·(∂a/∂z)·(∂z/∂w).

Example (x = 2, w = 0.5, b = 0, y = 1): z = 1, a = σ(1) = 0.7311, L = 0.0723. ∂L/∂a = 2(a − y) = −0.538, ∂a/∂z = a(1 − a) = 0.1966, ∂z/∂w = x = 2, so ∂L/∂w = (−0.538)(0.1966)(2) ≈ −0.2115.

The backward pass applies this layer by layer from the output to the input, reusing the values stored in the forward pass. If a weight affects the loss through two paths, the two contributions are added.

P27What is the primary goal of backpropagation?2 marks

The goal of backpropagation is to compute the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight and bias in the network, efficiently, in one backward sweep using the chain rule.

  • These gradients tell gradient descent which way, and how much, to change each parameter to reduce the loss.
  • It reuses the values saved in the forward pass, so the cost is about the same as one forward pass instead of one extra pass per weight.
  • Its final purpose is to minimise the error between predicted and actual output.

P28How does backpropagation update the weights and biases in a neural network?2 to 5 marks

Backpropagation finds the gradients; gradient descent then applies them: θ ← θ − η·∂L/∂θ for every weight and bias.

  1. Forward pass: compute z and the activation layer by layer, store them, and compute the loss.
  2. Output error: for sigmoid + binary cross-entropy, ∂L/∂z = ŷ − y (for softmax + cross-entropy it is also ŷ − y).
  3. Propagate backwards: ∂L/∂W = (error at this layer) × (previous layer's activation), ∂L/∂b = error; pass the error to the previous layer through the weights and the activation derivative.
  4. Update every weight and bias with the rule above.

Worked example (one sigmoid neuron, x = 2, w = 0.4, b = 0.1, y = 1, η = 0.1):

P29Describe the process of forward and backward propagation in a neural network, emphasising their roles in training the deep model.5 to 7 marks

Training repeats a loop of four steps: forward propagation, loss, backward propagation, weight update.

  x --> [Layer 1] --> [Layer 2] --> [Output] --> y-hat --> Loss(y-hat, y)
        <-- dL/dW1 <-- dL/dW2 <-- dL/dW3 <-- dL/dy-hat
  forward pass: compute and store z, a      backward pass: chain rule

How to sketch: boxes left to right for layers with arrows forward on top, and arrows backward underneath labelled with the gradients.

Forward propagation (role: make a prediction and measure the error):

  • Each neuron computes z = Σwx + b and a = f(z); the outputs of one layer become the inputs of the next; the last layer gives ŷ (softmax or sigmoid).
  • The loss L(ŷ, y) says how wrong the prediction is. All z and a values are cached.

Backward propagation (role: find how much each parameter contributed to the error):

  • Start at the output with ∂L/∂z = ŷ − y, then move backwards: ∂L/∂W = error × previous activation, and pass the error back through the weights and f′(z) (for ReLU, multiply by 1 if z > 0 else 0).
  • Then update θ ← θ − η·∂L/∂θ.

Worked example (the course network: 2 inputs, two ReLU hidden layers of 2, one sigmoid output; x = (0.5, 0.8), y = 1, η = 0.1):

  • Forward: hidden 1 = (0.375, 0.440), hidden 2 = (0.548, 0.630), z₃ = 0.838, ŷ = σ(0.838) ≈ 0.698, loss = −log(0.698) ≈ 0.359.
  • Backward: ∂L/∂z₃ = 0.698 − 1 = −0.302; ∂L/∂w₃ = (−0.302×0.548, −0.302×0.630) = (−0.1655, −0.1901).
  • Update: w₃ = 0.60 − 0.1(−0.1655) = 0.6165 and 0.65 − 0.1(−0.1901) = 0.6690; b₃ = 0.10 + 0.0302 = 0.1302.

Why both are needed: forward propagation produces the prediction and the loss, backward propagation turns the loss into gradients for every weight, and the update uses them; without the backward pass the many parameters of a deep model could not be trained efficiently.

P32What are the two main components of an autoencoder? What is an autoencoder and how is it used in unsupervised learning tasks?2 marks

An autoencoder is a neural network trained to reproduce its own input through a narrow hidden layer. It needs no labels, so it is unsupervised.

  input x --> [ ENCODER ] --> code h --> [ DECODER ] --> output x-hat
              h = f(x)      (bottleneck)    x-hat = g(h)
  loss = || x - x-hat ||^2  (reconstruction error)
  • Encoder: compresses the input into a code h = f(x).
  • Decoder: reconstructs the input from the code, x̂ = g(h).

Uses: dimensionality reduction and compression, feature learning (the code feeds a classifier), denoising, anomaly detection (large reconstruction error flags unusual data). Example: a 784 → 32 → 784 autoencoder has (784×32 + 32) + (32×784 + 784) = 50,992 parameters.

P33What is an Under-complete Autoencoder? Explain layer-by-layer pre-training of an autoencoder.3 to 5 marks

An under-complete autoencoder has a code smaller than the input (h < n). The bottleneck cannot copy the input, so it must keep only the most important features. With linear layers it behaves like PCA; with non-linear activations it learns a more powerful compression.

Layer-wise pre-training builds a deep network one layer at a time:

  1. Train autoencoder 1 on the raw input x to reconstruct x. Keep its code h₁ and discard its decoder.
  2. Train autoencoder 2 on h₁ to reconstruct h₁. Keep its code h₂.
  3. Repeat for as many layers as needed.
  4. Stack the trained encoders and add an output (classifier) layer on top.
  5. Fine-tune the whole stack with ordinary backpropagation on the labelled task.
  Step 1:  x  -> [ 256 ] -> x-hat      keep encoder 1, drop decoder
  Step 2:  h1 -> [  64 ] -> h1-hat     keep encoder 2, drop decoder
  Step 3:  x -> 256 -> 64 -> [10 softmax]   fine-tune all layers

Example sizes: 784 → 256 → 64 → 10 has (784×256 + 256) + (256×64 + 64) + (64×10 + 10) = 200,960 + 16,448 + 650 = 218,058 parameters.

Why: each layer starts from a sensible initialization instead of random weights, and unlabelled data can be used for pre-training. Before ReLU and batch normalization made deep networks easy to train this was important.

P39How do padding and stride affect CNN performance?3 marks

Output-size formula: W_(out) = ⌊(W − F + 2P)/S⌋ + 1, where W is the input size, F the kernel size, P the padding and S the stride.

Numeric example (W = 7, F = 3):

Padding PStride SCalculationOutput size

11(7 − 3 + 2)/1 + 17 (same size)
02⌊(7 − 3)/2⌋ + 13

  • Padding (adding a border of zeros): without it the map shrinks at every layer (F − 1 pixels lost) and border pixels are used less. With P = (F − 1)/2 and S = 1 ("same" padding, for example P = 1 for a 3×3 kernel) the size is kept, so many layers can be stacked and the edges are preserved. Cost: slightly more computation and zero-valued border values.
  • Stride (step of the kernel): a larger stride reduces the output size and the computation, and gives a larger receptive field quickly, but it can skip fine detail and may lower accuracy. Example: a 32×32×3 input, 16 filters of 3×3, P = 1: stride 1 gives 32×32×16 and 16×27×32×32 = 442,368 multiplications; stride 2 gives 16×16×16 and 110,592, four times fewer.
  • AlexNet's first layer uses stride 4 to reduce 227×227 to 55×55 at once.

Summary: padding preserves spatial information and size; stride trades detail for speed and memory.

P41Why is a deep neural network with one or more hidden layers necessary for XOR gate implementation?2 marks

A single neuron draws one straight line, so it can only separate linearly separable classes. XOR is not: the two 1-outputs (0,1) and (1,0) lie on opposite corners, and no single line puts them apart from (0,0) and (1,1).

Proof in one line: a neuron would need b < 0 for (0,0), w₁ + b ≥ 0 and w₂ + b ≥ 0 for the two 1-rows, and w₁ + w₂ + b < 0 for (1,1). Adding the middle two gives w₁ + w₂ + 2b ≥ 0, so w₁ + w₂ + b ≥ −b > 0, which contradicts the last condition.

A hidden layer fixes this: the hidden neurons transform the inputs into a new space (h₁ = OR, h₂ = AND) where the classes are separable by one line, and the output neuron draws it. Non-linear hidden units are what let the network bend the decision boundary.

P46Describe the concepts of regularization and dropout layers, highlighting their importance in improving model performance and preventing overfitting.7 marks

Overfitting: the model memorises the training data (low training error, high validation error) because it is too flexible for the amount of data. Regularization is any technique that reduces this variance and improves generalisation to unseen data.

TechniqueHow it works
L2 (weight decay)Loss + (λ/2)Σw²; every update pulls weights gently towards zero, giving a smoother function
L1Loss + λΣ|w|; pushes many weights to exactly 0 (sparse model)
DropoutRandomly switch off neurons during training (below)
Early stoppingStop at the epoch where validation error is lowest
Data augmentationFlips, crops and rotations enlarge the effective data set

Dropout layer. During training, each neuron in the layer is set to zero with probability p (for example 0.5 in the AlexNet FC layers; 0.2 to 0.5 is typical). A different thinned network is used for every mini-batch.

  1. Draw a random 0/1 mask for the layer (1 = keep with probability 1 − p).
  2. Multiply the activations by the mask.
  3. Scale the survivors by 1/(1 − p) ("inverted dropout") so the expected activation is unchanged.
  4. At test time use the full network with no dropout and no extra scaling.

Example: h = [2, 4, 6, 8], p = 0.5, mask = [1, 0, 1, 0]. After the mask [2, 0, 6, 0]; after scaling by 1/(1 − 0.5) = 2 the output is [4, 0, 12, 0]. With 10 hidden neurons and p = 0.4, about 6 are active and 4 dropped in each training pass.

Why it helps: no neuron can rely on a particular partner being present, so the network learns more robust features. With M neurons there are 2^M possible thinned networks, so training with dropout acts like averaging a huge ensemble at the cost of one network.

Importance: they close the gap between training and validation error (the signature of overfitting) without needing more data. The strength (λ or p) is a hyperparameter tuned on the validation set; too much causes underfitting.

A. Steps in designing a deep neural network

  1. Define the task and the metric: regression, binary or multi-class? Decide what "good" means (MSE, accuracy, AUC).
  2. Split the data into train / validation / test before anything else (for example 64 / 16 / 20%).
  3. Preprocess: scale or normalise the inputs (pixels to [0, 1]).
  4. Baseline architecture: start small, for example 2 hidden layers of 50 neurons; grow only if it underfits.
  5. Hidden activation and initialization: ReLU with He initialization (tanh or sigmoid with Xavier).

  7. Optimizer and learning rate: Adam is a sound default; mini-batch of 32, 64 or 128; add a learning-rate schedule if needed.
  8. Regularization: L2, dropout, batch normalization, augmentation, early stopping as needed.
  9. Train and monitor training against validation error; never touch the test set until the end.
  10. Diagnose and tune: high train and high validation error means high bias (bigger model, train longer); low train and high validation means high variance (more data, regularize).

P51Why is non-linearity important in neural networks?3 marks

Without a non-linear activation, any number of layers collapses into a single linear layer, so the network could only learn straight-line (linear) relationships.

Proof: W₂(W₁x + b₁) + b₂ = (W₂W₁)x + (W₂b₁ + b₂), which is again of the form Wx + b. Scalar example: weights 3 and −2 give −2·(3x) = −6x, always a straight line.

With a ReLU between them, f(x) = −2·ReLU(3x) is 0 for x < 0 and −6x for x > 0, a bent line that no single linear layer can produce.

  • Real data (images, speech, text) is not linearly separable; XOR is the classic case a single linear unit cannot solve.
  • Non-linear units let each layer bend the decision boundary, so depth adds representational power. With enough hidden units a single hidden layer can approximate any continuous function (universal approximation theorem).
  • Non-linearities such as ReLU also keep gradients healthy (derivative 1 for z > 0), whereas a step function has zero gradient and cannot be trained with backpropagation.
