Unit 1: Computer Networks and the Internet

Question Bank — CO1

All 20 questions, as given in the official question bank

Q1Define computer network. L1 · 2 marks

A collection of interconnected computers and devices that can exchange data and share resources with each other, using a shared set of communication protocols.

Q2Explain the importance of protocols in networking. L2 · 4 marks

Protocols are the common "language" that lets devices with different hardware/software communicate reliably — they define formatting, error handling, and processing rules, without which devices couldn't understand each other's data at all.

Q3Differentiate between network edge and network core. L2 · 6 marks

The edge is the boundary where end-user devices connect (moderate speed, security-focused: firewalls/NAT/VPN); the core is the high-speed backbone that moves data between networks (very high speed, focused on routing/forwarding). See the comparison table on the Unit 1 notes page.

Q4Explain packet switching and circuit switching. L2 · 6 marks

Circuit switching reserves a dedicated, fixed path/bandwidth for the entire duration of a call (e.g. traditional telephony) — reliable but wastes capacity when idle. Packet switching breaks data into packets that travel independently and share link capacity on demand (the Internet's approach) — more efficient, but subject to variable delay and possible packet loss under congestion.

Q5Describe the layered architecture of the Internet. L2 · 6 marks

The Internet is organized as a stack of layers (OSI: physical, data link, network, transport, session, presentation, application), each layer providing services to the one above and relying on the one below, so protocols at each layer can be designed and evolved independently.

Q6Calculate transmission delay with a suitable example. L3 · 6 marks

Transmission Delay = packet size (bits) / bandwidth (bps). Worked example (1000-byte packet, 1 Mbps link → 8 ms) on the Numericals page.

Q7Calculate propagation delay in a communication link. L3 · 6 marks

Propagation Delay = distance / propagation speed. Worked example (2000 km at 2×10⁸ m/s → 10 ms) on the Numericals page.

Q8Explain throughput with a suitable example. L2 · 4 marks

Throughput is the actual data successfully delivered per unit time — always ≤ bandwidth, since latency, packet loss, and congestion all eat into the theoretical maximum. Worked example (760 Kbps effective throughput out of a nominally faster link, due to 5% packet loss) on the Numericals page.

Q9Analyze the causes of packet loss in networks. L4 · 8 marks

Three main causes: congestion (routers drop packets when overwhelmed), hardware failure (faulty routers/switches/cables/NICs), and buffer overflow (incoming packets exceed buffer capacity and the excess is discarded).

Q10Compare delay and throughput parameters. L4 · 8 marks

Delay measures how long a packet takes to arrive (ms); throughput measures how much data actually gets through per unit time (bps). A network can have low delay but low throughput (e.g. a fast but narrow link) or vice versa — they capture different dimensions of performance.

Q11Hospital network scenario — evaluate throughput/delay/packet-loss impact on monitoring reliability. L4 · 6 marks

Real-time patient monitoring needs low, predictable delay and near-zero packet loss (a lost vital-sign reading can't simply be "resent later" without risk); office traffic can tolerate more delay/loss. Mixed traffic without prioritization (QoS) risks monitoring data queuing behind bulk office traffic — argue for traffic prioritization/QoS to protect the monitoring stream.

Q12Illustrate packet flow through the network core. L3 · 6 marks

A packet enters via an edge router, the core's routers use protocols (OSPF within an AS, BGP between ISPs) to determine the best path, and switches forward it along that path — often with multiple redundant paths available in case one fails.

Q13Analyze the impact of congestion in packet-switched networks. L4 · 8 marks

Congestion causes router buffers to fill and overflow (packet loss), increases queuing delay for every packet on the path, and triggers retransmissions that add even more traffic — a feedback loop that can make congestion worse if not controlled (see TCP congestion control in Unit 3).

Q14Evaluate Internet performance measures. L5 · 6 marks

No single measure tells the whole story: delay captures responsiveness, packet loss captures reliability, and throughput captures actual capacity delivered. Real evaluation requires looking at all three together, in the context of what the application needs (e.g. video calls prioritize low delay; file transfer prioritizes throughput).

Q15Campus network redesign scenario — design a scalable architecture. L6 · 6 marks

Layer the design around edge (Wi-Fi APs/switches sized for concurrent hybrid-learning users), a redundant edge-to-core path (avoiding single points of failure), and bandwidth allocation via QoS so cloud-application and video traffic don't starve each other during peak hours.

Q16Multinational video-conferencing latency scenario — analyze causes, suggest fixes. L4 · 6 marks

Likely causes: long propagation delay (geographic distance), queuing delay from congested international links, or insufficient bandwidth causing transmission delay. Fixes: route via geographically closer data centers/CDNs, apply QoS to prioritize video traffic, and provision more bandwidth on the bottleneck link.

Q17Gaming platform packet-loss scenario — examine switching/congestion role, recommend solutions. L5 · 6 marks

Packet-switched networks share bandwidth on demand, so a surge of simultaneous players causes congestion → buffer overflow → packet loss. Solutions: traffic prioritization (QoS) for real-time game traffic, load balancing across servers, and provisioning additional capacity for peak hours.

Q18Design a simple network architecture for a college campus. L6 · 5 marks

Access layer (Wi-Fi APs/Ethernet in classrooms/hostels) → edge layer (edge routers/switches with firewall, NAT) → core layer (high-speed backbone connecting buildings) → ISP uplink — with redundant links at the core for reliability.

Q19ISP core-congestion during live streaming — propose an optimized architecture. L6 · 8 marks

Use CDNs/edge caching so streaming content is served from servers near users instead of crossing the congested core repeatedly, add redundant high-bandwidth core paths, and apply congestion-aware routing (e.g. OSPF/BGP path selection) to spread load across multiple routes.

Q20Create a diagram showing the Internet communication process. L6 · 10 marks

Diagram: User's device → Access layer (Wi-Fi/Ethernet) → Edge router (Network Edge) → ISP/Core network (routing via OSPF/BGP) → Destination server, with the response following the same path in reverse — matches the "How Data Travels" example on the Unit 1 notes page.
